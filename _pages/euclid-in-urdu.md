In the second half of the nineteenth century, Euclid's *Elements* was read, translated and taught in Urdu in North India. The central figure is [Munshi Zakaullah](https://www.rekhta.org/authors/munshi-mohammad-zakaullah/profile) (1832–1910). He studied and then taught mathematics at Delhi College, and from 1872 to 1887 was professor at Muir Central College in Allahabad, teaching Western science through Urdu. His mathematical and educational writings formed part of the Aligarh movement, the reform initiative that reconfigured Muslim education by incorporating Western scientific and mathematical learning. Urdu was its principal medium of instruction. His Euclid translations and pedagogical writings were used in classrooms at institutions such as Delhi College, Muir Central College and the Oriental College in Lahore. The translations drew primarily on English editions, including those of [Isaac Todhunter](https://archive.org/search?query=Todhunter+Elements+of+Euclid) and [Robert Potts](https://archive.org/search?query=Potts+Euclid%27s+Elements+of+Geometry). Persian and Arabic Euclidean traditions continued to circulate in South Asia alongside them.

The question is how a text long central to European educational life acquired new meanings when rendered into Urdu, embedded in reformed curricula and mobilised within Muslim reformist visions of modern education. And how, in turn, these practices reshaped both Euclidean geometry and local mathematical cultures.

Some of the surrounding history has been written. Benjamin Wardhaugh's [*Reading Euclid*](https://www.history.ox.ac.uk/reading-euclid) project, with [Philip Beeley and Yelda Nasifoglu](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.4269490), reframed the *Elements* in early modern Britain and Europe as a historically situated text. It was shaped by reading, annotation, pedagogy and material production, not handed down as a timeless authority. For South Asia, [Gregg De Young](https://doi.org/10.1006/hmat.1995.1014) and Sonja Brentjes have examined elite encounters with Euclid mediated through Persian and Arabic mathematical traditions in the late medieval and early modern periods. Gail Minault and [Dhruv Raina](https://www.brepols.net/products/IS-9782503566047-1) have studied Euclid's institutionalisation in nineteenth-century colonial mathematics education, at Delhi College among other places. Karine Chemla's work on mathematical proof has shown how European scholars used Euclidean norms to evaluate non-European traditions. What remains is a sustained, text-centred history of Euclid in North Indian vernacular contexts, and in Urdu in particular. The corpus has received almost no attention as mathematics.

I have access to digitised editions of approximately thirty of Zakaullah's books: textbooks, translations and educational treatises. These will be supplemented by archival materials at Aligarh Muslim University connected to the Scientific Society and the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College. Colonial education reports, examination syllabi and the English-language Euclid textbooks used in Indian schools complete the picture. The aim is to show how the *Elements* functioned as a formative educational and cultural text in Muslim contexts, and to recover the active role of South Asian scholars in reshaping mathematical knowledge through translation, pedagogy and reform.

The Urdu chapter of Euclid's history is largely unwritten, and I am looking for people to write it with. Specifically: readers at home in nineteenth-century Urdu mathematical and scientific prose; historians of the Arabic and Persian Euclidean traditions, who will see borrowings I would miss; anyone with access to, or knowledge of, the archives at Aligarh Muslim University and the Punjab University library; historians of colonial education in India; and, further down the road, a collaborator for a bilingual edition of Zakaullah's Euclid, so that the book can be read again rather than merely studied.
